# Modelling a time series with slow and fast components: what actually works

Everything here is generated by `data.py` (a synthetic daily series: linear trend +
annual seasonality as the low-frequency part; weekly seasonality + short-memory
AR(1) noise + heteroskedastic noise as the high-frequency part — true components
kept around for diagnostics), `models.py` (five modelling strategies), and two
backtests: `experiment.py` (rolling one-step-ahead, 5-fold expanding-window
walk-forward) and `multistep.py` (a single 90-day-ahead forecast from a fixed
origin, no future information fed to anyone). Run both; plots land in `plots/`.

## The two backtests answer different questions, and that distinction is the
## most important finding here

**Rolling one-step-ahead** (`experiment.py`): at every test day, the true value
of every earlier day — including earlier days inside the test window — is a
legitimate feature. This is how a lot of production forecasting actually runs
(you observe today's true demand before forecasting tomorrow's).

**Fixed-origin multi-step** (`multistep.py`): you forecast a 90-day horizon from
today with nothing fed back. Lag-1 doesn't exist for day 2 of the horizon unless
you substitute your own prediction for it.

These two setups produce opposite rankings. Reporting only one of them is how
"decomposition doesn't help" or "just use XGBoost with lags" turns into bad
advice that fails in production.

## Result 1 — one-step-ahead: a single XGBoost with lags + calendar features wins

| method | RMSE | MAE | MASE (vs. seasonal-naive lag-7) |
|---|---|---|---|
| B: one XGBoost, raw target, slow+fast features combined | 1.50 | 1.06 | 0.38 |
| A: baseline XGBoost, raw target, short lags + calendar | 1.61 | 1.17 | 0.43 |
| C: two-stage, smoothed target then residual | 3.11 | 2.66 | 0.96 |
| D: MSTL classical decomposition + residual XGBoost | 3.20 | 2.59 | 0.93 |
| naive seasonal (lag-7) | 3.47 | 2.80 | 1.00 |
| E: two-stage, raw target with slow features only, then residual | 6.20 | 5.43 | 1.97 |

(`plots/metric_rmse.png`, `plots/metric_mae.png`, `plots/metric_mase.png`,
`plots/last_fold_overlay.png`, `plots/small_multiples.png`)

The un-decomposed models (A, B) beat every decomposition variant by 2-4x. This
is not a fluke of this dataset — it's the general reason gradient boosting on
lagged raw values is the default in short-horizon forecasting competitions
(M4/M5 winners were almost all lag+calendar-feature GBMs, not decomposition
pipelines). Two mechanisms explain it:

1. **A tree can express "low frequency AND high frequency" jointly without
   being told the split exists.** `lag_1` already encodes "yesterday's level,"
   which bakes in the current position of the annual cycle, the trend, and
   yesterday's noise realization all at once. Splitting the target into stages
   throws that joint information away and replaces it with a two-step estimate
   that has two sources of error instead of one.
2. **Every decomposition stage adds estimation error, and it compounds.**
   Stage 1 in C/D/E is never perfect — `plots/stage1_recovery_check.png` shows
   the smoothed-target stage-1 model tracking the true low-frequency curve
   closely near the fold's start and visibly drifting away by day ~60-90 of
   the fold (roughly: a rolling/EWMA-based level estimate lags a moving
   trend by design). Stage 2 then has to fit a residual that still contains
   stage 1's leftover bias, on top of the genuine unpredictable noise.

`plots/residual_acf_comparison.png` makes this concrete: the baseline's
residuals are white (no significant autocorrelation at any lag out to 21 days)
— there's nothing left to extract. The two-stage model's residuals still carry
significant autocorrelation at lag 1 and lag 7, meaning stage 2 didn't fully
absorb what stage 1 missed. **A whitened residual ACF is the right diagnostic
for "is a second stage worth adding," and here it says no.**

Method E (raw target restricted to slow features, then residual) is the
weakest of all — worse than doing nothing. Forcing stage 1 to fit the *raw,
noisy* target with only slow features gives it a bad, inconsistently-biased
fit (rolling-mean features lag a moving trend by different amounts depending
on where you are in the annual cycle — this shows up as one very bad fold out
of five in `plots/metrics.csv`, not five moderately bad ones). Smoothing the
target *first* (method C) before fitting stage 1 is a meaningfully better way
to do a two-stage split than fitting stage 1 to the raw target — if you're
going to decompose, smooth-then-residual beats slow-features-then-residual.

## Result 2 — fixed-origin, 90 days out: the ranking flips completely

(`multistep.py`, `plots/multistep_horizon_error.png`,
`plots/multistep_forecast_trajectory.png`)

| method | RMSE (90d) | RMSE (last 30d) |
|---|---|---|
| naive: repeat last known value | 8.1 | — |
| C: two-stage decomposition | 9.1 | 7.7 |
| A: baseline, recursive lag feedback | 24.7 | 25.1 |

The baseline model — the winner in Result 1 — is now the worst option, worse
than doing nothing. Fed its own predictions back in as `lag_1`, its errors
compound: by day ~20 of the horizon it has locked onto a fixed, wrong level
and oscillates around that instead of the real series (visible directly in
`plots/multistep_forecast_trajectory.png` — the red line settles ~15-20 units
too high and stays there). `plots/multistep_horizon_error.png` shows the
mechanism directly: the baseline's rolling error climbs sharply for the first
~20 days and then plateaus at a high level; it never recovers because every
future prediction is built on its own already-wrong recent output.

The two-stage model doesn't have this failure mode, because its slow stage
only needs features that are legitimately known at the forecast origin (time
index, calendar terms) plus rolling-window statistics *frozen at the origin*
— it never needs to guess a future lag to make a future prediction. Its
residual stage does still get recursively-fed pseudo-lags and does drift
somewhat, but the residual it's fitting is small and short-memory by
construction, so the drift stays bounded instead of compounding.

## Practical takeaway

There is no universally-best strategy — the right one depends on how far
ahead you actually need to forecast and whether the deployment setting gives
you fresh true values to condition on at each step:

- **If you get to observe the truth before each next-step forecast** (rolling
  1-step, "day-ahead" forecasting), skip decomposition. Fit one gradient
  boosted model on the raw target with short lags, differences, and calendar
  encodings (annual + weekly sin/cos, day-of-week). It's simpler, has one
  source of error instead of two, and beat every decomposition variant here.
- **If you must forecast a genuine multi-step horizon with nothing fed back**,
  do NOT let a lag-dependent tree model recurse on its own output across a
  long horizon — that's the one failure mode every other choice avoided.
  Decompose: model the slow component with features that are deterministically
  known for future dates (calendar terms, a time index) or frozen at the
  forecast origin (rolling statistics), and only let the fast/residual
  component use recursive lags, since a short-memory residual's compounding
  error stays bounded while the raw series' does not.
- **Whichever way you decompose, smooth the target before fitting stage 1**;
  don't fit stage 1 to the noisy raw target with a restricted feature set and
  hope subtraction cleans it up. The bias that leaves behind is inconsistent
  across the data (worse in some periods than others) in a way a clean
  smoother avoids.
- **Check the residual ACF, not just the aggregate error.** It's the
  diagnostic that tells you directly whether a second stage has anything left
  to do.
- If you reach for classical decomposition (STL/MSTL) as your slow-component
  model, use the multi-seasonal variant (MSTL) when more than one seasonal
  period is present — plain STL has no slot for a second period, and it
  leaks into whichever component you did specify. And watch trend
  extrapolation: a linear fit on the trend's tail is fine when the trend
  really is linear near the forecast origin, and dangerous (see the initial,
  discarded version of this experiment, where a slow multi-year wave was
  folded into "trend") whenever the slow component itself has curvature at
  the point you're extrapolating from.
